## T-J12｜同题六语矩阵 · English

### 第 1 轮（English Original）

The box was marked *KITCHEN MISC* in black Sharpie, though the handwriting wasn't Arthur’s. It was Clara’s, from the move before last, when they had left the two-story colonial in Evanston for the townhouse with the ground-floor master bedroom. Now the townhouse was sold too, and Arthur was going into the two-room apartment at St. Jude’s Assisted Living, where the kitchenette came with a microwave and a two-burner induction plate that turned itself off if you left a pot on it for more than twenty minutes.

"What’s in that one, Dad?" his daughter, Sarah, asked from the hallway. She was peeling blue painter's tape off the doorframe so the movers wouldn't scrape the trim with the sofa. "If it's the fondue pot, Goodwill doesn't even take those anymore."

"I'm looking," Arthur said.

He sat on a folding chair in the middle of the bare living room and cut through the brittle packing tape with his house key. Inside, nested in yellowed pages of the *Chicago Tribune* from October 2014, were things neither of them had needed for eight years: three mismatched corncob holders, a nutmeg grater, a set of measuring spoons missing the quarter-teaspoon, and at the very bottom, wrapped in a red linen dish towel, a heavy cast-iron skillet, six inches across.

Arthur lifted it out. His wrist dipped slightly with the weight.

He had forgotten he still owned it. In fact, if someone had asked him yesterday, he would have sworn he had left it behind at the cabin on Upper Silver Lake the summer they sold the boat.

He unwrapped the red towel. The skillet wasn't rusty. Years ago, someone—Arthur himself, probably, on some quiet Sunday afternoon he no longer remembered—had rubbed it down with flaxseed oil and baked it until the iron took on a dull,ebony sheen. Turned upside down, the bottom bore the faint, pitted imprint of the foundry: *GRISWOLD No. 3, ERIE PA.*

"Oh, Lord," Sarah said, walking in and wiping her forehead with the back of her wrist. "The little egg pan. I thought we lost that."

"So did I," Arthur said.

He ran his thumb along the pour spout on the left side. There was a tiny nick in the iron lip, no wider than a matchhead. In 1978, during the great blizzard that buried the cars up to their antennas, the power had gone out on Elm Street for four days. Sarah had been six, burning up with an ear infection, crying because she wanted warm milk. Arthur had tried to chip a block of frozen milk out of the carton on the back porch using the edge of this skillet, and had struck the iron railing instead.

Later, when Clara got sick the first time and couldn't keep anything down except scrambled eggs cooked in butter until they were barely set, this was the only pan Arthur used. A twelve-inch skillet was too big for two eggs; they spread out too thin and dried at the edges. In the Griswold No. 3, two eggs came up thick and soft, like a yellow cloud.

"You can't take cast iron to St. Jude's, Dad," Sarah said gently. She knelt beside his chair and began re-wrapping the nutmeg grater in the newspaper. "The induction burner there needs flat stainless steel, and anyway, it's too heavy for your shoulder when you're washing up at that little sink."

Arthur looked at the pan in his lap. Through the front window, the moving van was backing up the driveway, its reverse beep cutting through the quiet October air.

"Does your stove in Naperville have gas?" Arthur asked.

"Yes," Sarah said. "Why?"

Arthur folded the red linen dish towel neatly over the handle and held the little skillet out to her, handle first.

"Don't put it in the dishwasher," he said. "Just hot water and a stiff brush. And dry it on the burner for a minute after, or the bottom will pit."

Sarah looked at the iron handle, then up at her father. She took it with both hands.

"Two eggs," she said. "Low heat, take them off before they look done."

"That's right," Arthur said, and stood up to let the movers in.

### 第 2 轮（中文翻译）

纸箱外头用黑色记号笔写着“厨房杂物”，但那不是亚瑟的字迹，是克拉拉写的——还是上一次搬家时留下的。那回他们卖掉了埃文斯顿的双层殖民地风格老宅，搬进了这栋主卧在一楼的联排别墅。如今这栋联排别墅也卖掉了，亚瑟即将住进圣裘德老年公寓的两居室。那里的迷你厨房配了一台微波炉和一个双眼电磁炉，锅在上面放满二十分钟就会自动断电。

“爸，那箱里装的是什么？”女儿莎拉在走廊里问。她正把贴在门框上的蓝色美纹纸胶带撕下来，免得搬家工人抬沙发时刮花木线条，“要是那口奶酪火锅，现在连旧货慈善店都不收了。”

“我正看呢。”亚瑟说。

他坐在空旷客厅中央的一把折叠椅上，用家门钥匙划开已经发脆的封箱胶带。箱子里衬着二〇一四年十月的旧《芝加哥论坛报》，纸页已经发黄，里面裹着些他们老两口八年都没碰过的零碎：三支不成对的玉米叉、一个肉豆蔻擦丝器、一套缺了四分之一茶匙的量勺，而在最底下，用一条红亚麻擦碗巾包着的，是一口沉甸甸的六英寸铸铁平底煎锅。

亚瑟把它拿了出来。手腕被那分量坠得往下沉了一下。

他早忘了自己还留着这口锅。要是昨天有人问起，他准保会发誓说，卖掉游艇的那个夏天，自己早就把它留在上银湖畔的度假木屋里了。

他解开红擦碗巾。煎锅一点儿没生锈。许多年前，不知是谁——多半是亚瑟自己在某个早已记不起的安静周日午后——在锅身上薄薄抹了一层亚麻籽油，放进烤箱里烘出了乌木般温润的暗光。把锅翻过来，锅底还留着铸造厂模糊斑驳的铭文：**宾州伊利，格里斯沃尔德3号**。

“天哪，”莎拉走进客厅，用手腕背面擦了擦额头，“是那口煎蛋小锅。我还以为早丢了呢。”

“我也以为丢了。”亚瑟说。

他的拇指顺着左侧的导流嘴摸过去。铁锅沿上有一个火柴头大小的微小豁口。一九七八年那场大暴风雪把路边的汽车一直埋到了天线顶，榆树街停了整整四天电。那年莎拉才六岁，因为中耳炎发着高烧，哭着要喝热牛奶。亚瑟去后门廊想用这口铁锅的边沿把冻成冰坨的盒装牛奶敲下一块来，结果失手磕在了铁栏杆上。

再后来，克拉拉头一回生病化疗，吃什么吐什么，唯独能咽下用黄油炒得刚断生、嫩滑滑的炒鸡蛋。那段时间亚瑟天天只用这口小锅。十二英寸的大平底锅炒两个鸡蛋太大了，蛋液摊得太薄，边上容易焦；唯有在这口格里斯沃尔德3号小锅里，两个鸡蛋炒出来又厚又软，像一团黄澄澄的云朵。

“爸，铸铁锅可不能带去圣裘德公寓，”莎拉温和地说。她在父亲椅边蹲下身，把那个肉豆蔻擦丝器重新包回旧报纸里，“那儿的电磁炉得用平底不锈钢锅，再说了，那儿的小水槽那么窄，你端着这么沉的铁锅刷洗，肩膀可受不了。”

亚瑟低头看着膝上的小铁锅。透过前窗，搬家公司的大货车正在往车道上倒车，倒车提示音划破了十月安静的空气。

“你在内珀维尔家里的灶台，烧的是燃气吧？”亚瑟问。

“是啊，”莎拉说，“怎么了？”

亚瑟把红亚麻擦碗巾整整齐齐地叠在锅柄上，把锅柄朝前递向女儿。

“别放进洗碗机，”他说，“用热水和硬毛刷刷就行。洗完了搁炉火上烘一分钟，不然锅底容易生锈起麻点。”

莎拉看了看那根铁锅柄，又抬头看了看父亲，伸出双手接了过去。

“两个鸡蛋，”她说，“小火，看着还没全熟的时候就出锅。”

“没错。”亚瑟说，随即站起身，去给搬家工人开门。
